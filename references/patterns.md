# Patterns

The full catalogue of tells, merged from humanizer 3.1.0 (H, with its section number), stop-slop (S) and no-ai-slop (N), with duplicates folded together. Patterns run strongest first inside eight families.

Tier 1: act on one sighting. Tier 2: act when the instance clearly fits. Tier 3, weak alone: act only when other tells share the passage. Hard rule: SV's house rule, never relaxed. Strict mode treats every pattern as tier 1.

Every example keeps the facts of its "Before" and adds none. Where a fix needs a fact only SV has, the example shows an `[ADD: ...]` placeholder instead of inventing one. Hinglish forms live in references/hinglish.md, LinkedIn forms in platforms/linkedin/guide.md.

## A. Staging instead of stating

The sentence signals importance instead of adding a fact. These are the strongest and most frequent tells in current model prose.

### 1. Binary contrasts and negative lists

Tier: hard rule for SV (tier 1 in the sources).
Watch for: not X but Y; not just (only, merely) X, but Y; it's not X, it's Y; X isn't the problem, Y is; the question (answer) isn't X, it's Y; not because X, because Y; it feels like X, it's actually Y; stops being X and starts being Y; doesn't mean X, it means Y; X rather than Y used only for weight; the contrast split across two sentences; a clipped negative tail (", no guessing"); negative lists ("Not a X. Not a Y. A Z.", "It wasn't X. It wasn't Y. It was Z.").
Why: The negative half denies something nobody claimed, so the positive half sounds bigger. It adds weight without adding a claim. Negative lists stretch the same move into a striptease.
Fix: State Y directly. A plain comparison where both halves carry information is fine ("X matters more than Y"). When a real belief needs correcting, name who holds it and give the evidence, without the formula.
Keep apart: a negative list denies what something is ("Not a X. Not a Y. A Z."). A row of literal facts in negative form ("No clients. No portfolio.") is #2.
Before:
> YouTube isn't a video platform. It's a search engine.

After:
> YouTube works like a search engine.

Before (negative list):
> Not a hack. Not a trick. A system.

After:
> It's a system.

Before (split across sentences):
> This doesn't mean you should stop making Shorts. It means Shorts can't carry the channel alone.

After:
> Shorts can't carry the channel alone, though that's no reason to stop making them.

### 2. Closers, fragments and emphasis crutches

Tier: 1.
Watch for: a one-sentence paragraph that restates the one before; "That's the real win."; "That's it. That's the whole strategy."; "Read that again."; "Let that sink in."; "Full stop."; "Period."; "Make no mistake."; "This matters because"; the same closer after several sections; a sentence after an example that names what it showed ("This shows the importance of...", "The lesson was clear:"); a row of fragments ("No budget. No team. No excuses."); "X. And Y. And Z."; one word in ALL CAPS or split by periods (every. single. day.); every paragraph ending on a punchy one-liner.
Why: The line asks the reader to pause on a claim instead of adding to it.
Fix: Cut a closer that repeats the point or explains an example the reader just saw. Keep a short sentence when it adds a new fact or consequence. Merge a row of fragments into one sentence with a specific claim. Vary how paragraphs end.
Before:
> We changed one thing. The thumbnail. That's it. That's the whole strategy.

After:
> We changed only the thumbnail.

Before:
> Uploaded every week for a year. Every. Single. Week. Read that again.

After:
> I uploaded every week for a year.

### 3. Sayings, quotable lines and kickers

Tier: 1.
Watch for: aphorisms and pull-quote lines ("Your network is your net worth.", "Consistency compounds."); X is the Y of Z; X is the new Y; X becomes a trap; the language of, the currency of, the architecture of; the real question is; at its core; what really matters; fundamentally; the heart of the matter; a last line that turns the point into a metaphor or a mic drop ("The future isn't coming. It's already here.").
Why: An ordinary point is dressed as a hidden truth. The dressing adds no detail, and readers have seen the shape many times.
Fix: In the middle of a piece, replace the saying with the specific claim. At the end, delete the kicker. Do not rewrite it into a better metaphor and do not keep its rhythm. End on the clearest concrete sentence already in the piece; if the ending needs closure, add a plain takeaway or next step. Test: if a line would look good on a poster, rewrite it.
Before:
> Your thumbnail is a promise. Your video is the delivery.

After:
> The video has to deliver what the thumbnail shows.

Before (kicker):
> We cut the intro to eight seconds and watch time doubled. Because in the end, attention is the new currency.

After:
> We cut the intro to eight seconds and watch time doubled.

### 4. Throat-clearing and staged run-ups

Tier: 1.
Watch for: Here's the thing; Here's what (why, how) as an opener; Let's dive in; Let's break it down; Let me be clear; The truth is; The uncomfortable truth is; It turns out; I'll be honest; I'm going to be honest; Can we talk about; a standalone "Honestly?"; "Look," as an opener; Real talk; Quick note; Heads up; Without further ado; Now let's look at.
Why: The writer announces the point, or stages a moment of candour, instead of making the point.
Fix: Delete the run-up and start with the point. "Honestly" or "look" inside a casual sentence is ordinary speech; the tell is the standalone opener before a routine claim.
Before:
> Here's the thing about Shorts: they don't build loyal subscribers.

After:
> Shorts don't build loyal subscribers.

Before (staged candour):
> Is a channel audit worth it? Honestly? It depends on how often you upload.

After:
> Whether an audit is worth it depends on how often you upload.

### 5. Faux-insight setups

Tier: 1.
Watch for: What nobody tells you; Nobody talks about this; The part everyone misses; This is the part most people skip; What most people get wrong; Most people think X (with nobody named); 99% of creators don't know this; The secret is.
Why: The setup casts the writer as the lone expert and flatters the reader for being let in. The claim is rarely a secret.
Fix: Cut the setup and let the claim stand. If it really is uncommon knowledge, show why with evidence.
Before:
> What nobody tells you about growing on YouTube: the first 30 seconds decide everything.

After:
> The first 30 seconds decide whether people stay.

### 6. Rhetorical setups and self-answered questions

Tier: 1.
Watch for: What if I told you; Think about it; Here's what I mean; Plot twist; Spoiler; Guess what; a question answered in the next breath ("The result? 3x views.", "Why does this matter? Because..."); "And that's okay."
Why: The text performs a reveal instead of delivering the point. "And that's okay" grants permission nobody asked for.
Fix: Drop the setup and state the point. A real question to the reader is fine when the answer is theirs to give.
Before:
> What if I told you most channels don't need more uploads? Think about it.

After:
> Most channels don't need more uploads.

Before:
> The result? 3x the views.

After:
> It got 3x the views.

### 7. Arguing with no one

Tier: 1.
Watch for: This isn't about; I'm not saying; To be clear; Don't get me wrong; This is not to say; Some might say... but; You might think... but; A tempting approach would be; It would be easy to just.
Why: The text answers an objection nobody raised, often a leftover from an earlier draft.
Fix: Delete the defence. If it holds a real claim, state the claim. Keep an objection the text names and answers in full, and an option a reader would actually weigh. Several unrelated defences in a row are a stronger sign than one.
Before:
> I'm not saying thumbnails don't matter. But titles matter more.

After:
> Thumbnails matter, and titles matter more.

### 8. Colon reveals

Tier: 2.
Watch for: a noun phrase, a colon, then a dramatic reveal: "The best part: it learns.", "The secret sauce: consistency.", "One word: retention.", "The twist: nobody watched past ten seconds."
Why: The colon stages a reveal that a plain sentence could state.
Fix: Write the plain sentence. Use colons for lists, labels, times and quotes. After a colon, use sentence case unless a proper noun, title or code needs a capital.
Keep: SV's "Label: text" list lines. A label is not a reveal.
Before:
> The secret sauce: consistency.

After:
> Consistency did most of the work.

## B. Telling instead of showing

The text announces that something is important, hard or deep instead of showing it.

### 9. Telling the reader what to think

Tier: 1.
Watch for: This distinction matters; That last part matters more than it sounds; The key point is; As you can see; Notice that; This is important; This is genuinely hard; This is what leadership actually looks like; This is what X actually looks like; ...actually matters; a redundant "In other words".
Why: The label tells the reader to find something important, surprising or hard. If the prose already shows it, the label insults the reader. If it doesn't, the label is a promise with nothing behind it.
Fix: Delete the label when the point is clear. Otherwise replace it with the fact, example, mechanism or consequence that makes the point matter.
Before:
> This is the part that matters more than it sounds: we posted at the same time every week.

After:
> We posted at the same time every week.

### 10. Vague declaratives

Tier: 2.
Watch for: The reasons are structural; The implications are significant; The stakes are high; The consequences are real; This is the deepest problem; It's complicated; There's a lot to unpack.
Why: The sentence announces importance without naming the thing.
Fix: Name the specific reason, implication or stake from the source. If the source doesn't have it, cut the sentence or ask SV.
Before:
> The implications for small creators are significant.

After:
> (Cut, or name it: [ADD: what changes for small creators].)

### 11. Portable sentences

Tier: 2.
Watch for: sentences that would fit any person, channel, company, country or product unchanged: "Consistency is key.", "Content is king.", "Success doesn't happen overnight.", "Focus on providing value.", "Engagement is everything."
Why: A sentence that fits everyone says nothing about this subject.
Fix: Run the portability test on every generic sentence: could it move unchanged into someone else's post? If yes, cut it or replace it with a fact, example, mechanism, consequence or judgment specific to this subject. Those facts come only from SV: ask, or leave a placeholder.
Before:
> Consistency is key to growing on YouTube.

After:
> (Cut, or make it specific: [ADD: what consistency looked like on a real channel, with numbers].)

## C. Inflation and borrowed authority

The fact underneath is usually sound. Keep it and remove the dressing.

### 12. AI vocabulary and jargon

Tier: 1 for the cut-on-sight list in references/words.md; 2 for the rest of that file.
Watch for: the lists in references/words.md. Clusters are the strongest signal: two or three of these words in one paragraph.
Why: Models use these words far more often than people do.
Fix: Use the plain word or delete. A formal word that is on no list is fine.
Keep: technical and platform meanings, such as landscape video, API key, YouTube's key moments, highlights, gated content and robust statistics.
Before:
> Let's delve into how this robust strategy can elevate your channel.

After:
> This strategy can grow your channel.

### 13. Inflated significance and send-offs

Tier: 2.
Watch for: marks a pivotal moment; a testament to; plays a key (vital, crucial) role; shaping the future of; reflects a broader trend; enduring legacy; setting the stage for; evolving landscape; indelible mark; solidifies its position; underscores its significance; "Despite these challenges, it continues to thrive"; stock sections such as "Challenges and future outlook"; send-offs such as the future looks bright, exciting times ahead, this is just the beginning, onwards and upwards, a step in the right direction.
Why: An ordinary detail is said to mark a change, prove a legacy or promise a future.
Fix: Keep the fact and drop the significance. End on the last concrete fact. If SV has real plans, state those. Plain words such as "journey" are fine; the inflation is the tell.
Before:
> Hitting 10k subscribers marks a pivotal moment in my journey as a strategist. This is just the beginning!

After:
> My channel crossed 10k subscribers.

### 14. Shallow -ing riders

Tier: 2.
Watch for: an -ing clause bolted onto a fact: highlighting, underscoring, emphasizing, ensuring, reflecting, symbolizing, showcasing, contributing to, fostering, cultivating, encompassing.
Why: It pretends to explain what the fact means. Attaching it to a named source does not make it true.
Fix: Keep the fact. Keep the rider only when the source supports it, and then write it as its own clause with a real consequence.
Before:
> We launched a weekly series, highlighting our commitment to consistency.

After:
> We launched a weekly series.

### 15. Sales language

Tier: 2.
Watch for: stunning, breathtaking, must-watch, must-read, world-class, groundbreaking, renowned, nestled, in the heart of, rich (figurative), profound, exemplifies, commitment to, diverse array, unparalleled, next-level.
Why: The text reads like an advert, and readers discount adverts.
Fix: Say what the thing is and what it does. Let numbers and specifics do the selling.
Before:
> A stunning, must-watch breakdown of how creators really make money.

After:
> A breakdown of how creators make money.

### 16. Borrowed authority

Tier: 2.
Watch for: experts agree; experts argue; studies show; research says; industry reports suggest; many argue; widely regarded as; observers note; data shows (with no data); "featured in" or "as seen on" lists; follower counts used as proof.
Why: A name or an unnamed authority stands in for what was actually said.
Fix: Name the source and what it said, or cut the claim. If SV has no source, ask; never invent one. A missing citation alone is not a tell, since most writing is unsourced.
Before:
> Experts agree that Shorts eat into long-form views.

After:
> (Name it, [ADD: source and what it found], or cut the claim.)

### 17. Vague connections

Tier: 2.
Watch for: associated with; in association with; connected to; in connection with; linked to; tied to; involved with.
Why: The text says two things are connected without saying how.
Fix: Name the relationship the source gives. If the source doesn't say, keep the vague wording instead of inventing a role.
Before:
> She is associated with the channel, which she started in 2019.

After:
> She started the channel in 2019.

### 18. Fake-strong verbs

Tier: 2.
Watch for: serves as; stands as; functions as; acts as; represents; boasts; features; offers; maintains; made a decision; has the ability to; is able to; in order to.
Why: Simple verbs get swapped for longer, grander ones.
Fix: Use is, are, has, can and the direct verb.
Before:
> The channel serves as a hub for budget travel and boasts over 200 videos.

After:
> The channel is about budget travel and has over 200 videos.

Before:
> We made the decision to stop uploading Shorts.

After:
> We decided to stop uploading Shorts.

## D. Rhythm by rule

Shapes and punctuation applied everywhere, whether or not the meaning asks for them.

### 19. Forced triads

Tier: 2. Strict: two items beat three.
Watch for: three adjectives or nouns for rhythm ("simple, scalable and sustainable"); three parallel examples; three short facts followed by a lesson.
Why: Ideas arrive in threes to sound complete, whether or not the meaning has three parts.
Fix: Check that each item adds a distinct idea. Merge, keep the strongest, or vary the structure. Keep three when the content has three.
Before:
> Our process is simple, scalable and sustainable.

After:
> Our process is simple. (Keep any other item SV can back with a fact.)

### 20. Robotic rhythm

Tier: 2.
Watch for: several sentences in a row that start the same way; three sentences in a row of the same length; every paragraph built the same way (claim, example, punchline); stacked short punchy sentences; questions answered immediately, again and again.
Why: Shape applied by rule instead of by ear.
Fix: Merge sentences, change subjects, start with the action, mix short and long. A writer may repeat an opening on purpose once; keep that.
Before:
> I tested titles. I tested thumbnails. I tested upload times. I tested hooks.

After:
> I tested titles, thumbnails, upload times and hooks.

### 21. Dashes

Tier: hard rule for SV.
Watch for: em dashes (`—`), en dashes (`–`), spaced hyphens (` - `) and double hyphens (`--`) used as dashes.
Why: A dash lets the writer skip deciding how two clauses relate, so models put one everywhere. SV bans them outright.
Fix: Use a period, comma, colon or parentheses, or rewrite the sentence. Write ranges with "to" (10 to 20). Leave hyphens in compound words, code, commands, paths and URLs.
Before:
> The upload — our best so far — got 2x the views.

After:
> The upload, our best so far, got 2x the views.

### 22. Stacked qualifiers and reassurance hedges

Tier: 3.
Watch for: could potentially; might arguably; it's possible that it may; in some cases it may; to be fair; "Not always. Not perfectly. But..."
Why: Each round of editing adds one more hedge until every claim sounds unsure, usually to repair an earlier overstatement instead of reporting real doubt.
Fix: Keep one qualifier where the evidence needs it. Ordinary hedges such as "maybe" or "tends to" are human; keep them when the doubt is real.
Before:
> This could potentially help you possibly grow faster.

After:
> This may help you grow faster.

### 23. Synonym cycling

Tier: 3.
Watch for: rotating names for the same thing to avoid repeating a word ("The creator posted a Short. The YouTuber then...").
Why: The reader wonders whether these are different people or things. humanizer dropped this tell because people do it too; no-ai-slop keeps it. Here it counts as a clarity fix, weak alone as a tell.
Fix: If the clear word is right, repeat it, or use a pronoun.
Before:
> The creator posted a Short. The YouTuber then shared it as a Reel.

After:
> The creator posted a Short, then shared it as a Reel.

### 24. Hyphenated pairs after the noun

Tier: 3.
Watch for: high-quality, well-known, well-documented, long-term, real-time and data-driven placed after the noun.
Fix: Keep the hyphen before a noun ("a high-quality edit") and drop it after ("the edit is high quality"). Dictionary compounds such as third-party keep it everywhere.
Before:
> The edit is high-quality and the plan is long-term.

After:
> The edit is high quality and the plan is long term.

### 25. Lazy extremes

Tier: 3. Strict: always act.
Watch for: every, always, never, everyone, everybody, nobody, all, everything and 100% doing vague work.
Why: Sweeping words borrow authority, and readers think of the exception.
Fix: Use the real scope, or a number SV can back.
Keep: literal truths SV states ("I haven't missed an upload since March").
Before:
> Every creator struggles with titles.

After:
> Many creators struggle with titles.

## E. Agency and distance

The text hides who acted or hovers above the scene.

### 26. False agency

Tier: 2.
Watch for: inanimate things doing human verbs: the data tells us; the market decides; the decision emerged; a complaint became a fix; the culture shifts; the conversation moves toward; the numbers speak for themselves.
Why: It hides who acted. Models like it because it avoids naming a person.
Fix: Name the person who acted. If no specific person fits, use "you" or "we".
Keep: software that really acts, such as YouTube's recommendation system, can be the subject.
Before:
> The data told us to change the thumbnail.

After:
> We looked at the data and changed the thumbnail.

Before:
> A bad comment became a video idea.

After:
> I turned a bad comment into a video idea.

### 27. Passive voice and missing subjects

Tier: 3. Strict: no passive at all.
Watch for: "Mistakes were made", "It is believed that", "X was created", "No setup needed."
Why: It hides the actor and drains energy.
Fix: Find the actor and put them first. Keep the passive when the actor is unknown or truly irrelevant.
Before:
> Mistakes were made in the first 20 videos.

After:
> I made mistakes in the first 20 videos.

### 28. Narrator from a distance

Tier: 3. Strict: always act.
Watch for: "Nobody designed this.", "People tend to...", "This happens because..." and "This is why..." as a lecturer's opener; floating observations about "people" or "creators" from above.
Why: The writer hovers over the scene instead of putting the reader in it.
Fix: Put the reader in the room. "You" beats "people", and a scene beats a summary.
Before:
> Nobody plans to quit YouTube. It happens slowly.

After:
> You don't plan to quit YouTube; it happens slowly.

## F. Formatting by rule

Templates produce clean formatting too. The tell is decoration on every item.

### 29. Bold, labels and lists by rule

Tier: 2.
Watch for: bold on random phrases; list items that each open with a bold label the sentence then repeats ("Performance: Performance improved"); bullets where two sentences of prose read better; very short lists.
Why: Formatting goes on every item instead of where the content needs it.
Fix: No bold in anything SV will paste; most platforms won't render it. Use a list only when the content is a list. Labels stay when they carry information, which is SV's "Label: text" format. Turn label lists that restate themselves into prose.
Before:
> ✅ Titles: Titles were improved for search.
> ✅ Thumbnails: Thumbnails were redesigned.

After:
> We rewrote the titles for search and redesigned the thumbnails.

### 30. Decorative headings, emoji and arrows

Tier: 2.
Watch for: Title Case Headings; emoji in headings or as bullets (🚀 💡 ✅ 👉 🔥); arrows (→, 👇) as decoration; a horizontal rule between every section; headings over two-sentence sections; a heading written for effect instead of one that names the content.
Why: Decoration applied by rule.
Fix: Sentence case, no decorative emoji or arrows, headings only when the piece is long enough to need them. An emoji SV uses in their own voice (from samples) stays at SV's rate.
Before:
> 🚀 My Top 5 YouTube Growth Hacks 🚀

After:
> My top 5 YouTube growth hacks

### 31. Curly quotes

Tier: 3.
Watch for: curly quotes (“ ” ‘ ’) where the target format uses straight ones.
Fix: Match the target format. Phones curl quotes automatically, so this means nothing alone.

## G. Leftovers from the chat and the draft

Remove these outright.

### 32. Chatbot residue

Tier: hard rule.
Watch for: Great question; Certainly; Of course; Absolutely; You're absolutely right; I hope this helps; Let me know if you'd like; Would you like me to; Want me to; Here is a post for you; Feel free to; Happy posting.
Why: A chatbot's greeting, praise, offer or sign-off is left in text that should stand alone. It is the most certain tell.
Fix: Remove the wrapper and keep the content.
Before:
> Great question! Here's a LinkedIn post for you: [post] I hope this helps! Let me know if you'd like any changes.

After:
> [post]

### 33. Disclaimers and guesses

Tier: 1.
Watch for: as of my last update; while specific details are limited; based on available information; not publicly available; not widely documented; likely (grew up, earns, started) used to fill a gap; it is believed that; reportedly, with no report named.
Why: The text admits a gap, then fills it with a plausible guess.
Fix: State what the source doesn't show, or cut the sentence. Never fill the gap.
Before:
> While exact numbers aren't public, the channel likely earns around $10k a month.

After:
> The channel's earnings aren't public. (Or cut the sentence.)

### 34. Writing about the text

Tier: 2.
Watch for: In this post, I'll; Let me walk you through; The rest of this post explains; As we'll see; I want to explore; In this section; Hint:; the table below shows; a heading followed by a line that repeats it; an account of how the text was made ("compiled from", "generated with").
Why: The text describes itself instead of its subject.
Fix: Cut it and start with the subject. Keep a source credit the reader can follow, and a caveat that changes what the reader should do.
Before:
> In this post, I'll walk you through the three changes we made.

After:
> (Cut. Start with the first change.)

### 35. Recap endings

Tier: hard rule for SV.
Watch for: In conclusion; To sum up; Ultimately; Overall; So, to recap; At the end of the day; a TL;DR at the bottom; a last paragraph that restates the piece.
Why: The reader was just there. A recap signals that the writer doesn't trust the reader or the piece.
Fix: Delete it. End on the last concrete point, a plain next step, or one real question.
Before:
> In conclusion, packaging, retention and consistency are the pillars of growth.

After:
> (Cut. End on the last concrete point.)

## H. Writing for the wrong reader

### 36. Re-explaining what the reader knows

Tier: 2. Replies, comments, DMs and emails only.
Watch for: a reply that restates the problem, walks through the diagnosis and lays out the evidence before it reaches the answer; background the other person wrote; the answer sitting in the last line.
Why: A model writes for a reader with no context. In a reply the reader already has it, so rebuilding it buries the point.
Fix: Lead with the decision. Keep only the reasoning that would change whether the reader agrees, usually one fact they lack and any link they need to act. Act only when you can see the conversation or the text is plainly a reply.
Before:
> Great point! Posting frequency is an important factor, and many creators wonder about it. I looked at your last 10 videos: the ones with a full packaging pass got 3x the views. So yes, twice a week can work, as long as each video gets that pass.

After:
> Yes, twice a week works if each video still gets a full packaging pass. In your last 10 videos, those got 3x the views.
